# Access to Nature

Access to nature is about more than simply having a park nearby. The quality of that space, how safe it feels, whether it's physically accessible and whether people feel welcome all influence whether they can actually use it.

I've been interested in how access to green space differs between communities, and how things like income, location, disability and cultural background shape people's experiences of the outdoors.

## Barriers to access

### Distance, quality & safety

Having a green space nearby doesn't necessarily mean people can use or enjoy it.

Some of the research I've collected looks at:

- **Location:** How close people live to green spaces and whether nature is available within their neighbourhood.
    
- **Quality:** How well spaces are maintained, their biodiversity, facilities and overall condition.
    
- **Safety:** Fear of crime, antisocial behaviour, poor lighting and feeling uncomfortable or unsafe.
    
- **Physical accessibility:** Step-free routes, accessible paths, seating and public facilities.
    
- **Belonging:** Whether different communities feel represented, comfortable and welcome.
    
- **Time and energy:** Working hours, commuting and caring responsibilities can make outdoor time harder to fit into everyday life.
    
- **Transport and cost:** Distance, public transport and the cost of organised activities can all affect access.
    
- **Local investment:** Underfunding, poor maintenance, privatisation and restricted access can make nearby green spaces less usable.
    

These barriers don't affect everyone equally. Disabled people, older adults, women, families and marginalised communities can face additional challenges when trying to access outdoor spaces. [1, 2]

### Social & economic barriers

Access is also shaped by everyday circumstances. Even when green space is available, work, commuting, caring responsibilities and limited free time can make it difficult to spend time outdoors.

I'm interested in the difference between having access to nature in theory and actually being able to use it. A park might be geographically close, but that doesn't mean it's accessible, welcoming or practical for everyone.

## Environmental inequality

Access to green space isn't distributed equally. Income, ethnicity and where people live can all influence how much nature is available, as well as its quality. [3, 4]

Some of the reports I've collected explore the relationship between deprivation and access to green space. One idea that comes up is the existence of urban areas with very little accessible nature, sometimes described as 'grey deserts'. [3, 4]

It raises a bigger question: who benefits from urban green spaces, whose neighbourhoods receive investment, and why isn't access to nature treated as an essential part of public infrastructure?

## Nature, health & urban environments

I've also been looking at how unequal access to green space connects to wider health inequalities.

Some of the research explores:

- Mental health and psychological wellbeing.
    
- Air pollution and environmental exposure.
    
- Physical health and opportunities for activity.
    
- Differences in life expectancy between neighbourhoods.
    
- Health inequalities in deprived urban communities.
    

The Mental Health Foundation has a useful overview of the relationship between nature and mental health. [5] Other organisations mentioned in my research include The King's Fund, the Institute of Health Equity, British Heart Foundation, Friends of the Earth, Parks for London and Imperial College London's Environment Research Group.

I haven't matched every organisation to a specific statistic yet, so I'm keeping those references as research leads rather than treating them as evidence for the figures below.

### Green space and preventable deaths

Ngan et al. (2025) looked at how green space is distributed across UK neighbourhoods and how this relates to preventable deaths.

They found that more deprived urban areas in England, Scotland and Northern Ireland had less grassland. In their area-level models, a 1% increase in grassland was associated with 37% fewer annual preventable deaths in the most deprived urban areas of England. The corresponding estimates were 37% for five-year accumulated deaths in Northern Ireland and 41% in Scotland.

These figures are associations, not proof that increasing grassland would directly cause deaths to fall by those amounts. The authors also highlight the limitations of drawing conclusions about individuals from area-level data. Wales did not show the same significant pattern. [7]

This helped me trace the 37–41% figure from one of my original research screenshots. The screenshot had compressed several different estimates into one statement, so it's important not to present this as a single UK-wide statistic.

### Nature opportunities in schools

One of my research screenshots included a figure suggesting that only 24% of UK schools provide daily opportunities for children to connect with nature.

I've traced this to WWF's _Schools for Nature_ report (2024), which collected responses from teachers at 1,885 UK schools.

The report found that 24% offered daily opportunities for pupils to experience nature. There was also a difference between primary and secondary schools: 30% of primary schools offered these opportunities, compared with 12% of secondary schools.

There was a further gap between schools with different levels of deprivation. 52% of state schools with a low proportion of pupils receiving free school meals offered daily nature experiences, compared with 18% of schools with a high proportion.

These are school-reported survey findings, rather than direct observations of every pupil's experience. [8]

The original attribution on my Miro board, to the British Ecological Society English Policy Group and “Beal et al. (2025)”, doesn't match the report I've found. WWF's report, led by Melissa McKinlay and The Education Company, appears to be the source of the 24% figure.

### Air pollution and urban health

Green space isn't the only environmental factor affecting health in cities. Air pollution is another part of the picture.

A 2015 Guardian article reported on a King's College London health-impact assessment estimating that exposure to PM2.5 and nitrogen dioxide contributed to 9,416 early deaths in London in 2010.

The estimate was broken down into 3,537 deaths associated with PM2.5 and 5,879 associated with NO2. It's worth remembering that these were modelled population-level estimates, not deaths directly counted on death certificates. They're also based on 2010 pollution data, so shouldn't be presented as a current annual figure. [9]

I've also looked at research into London's Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), although the findings are a little more complicated than some of the original summaries suggested.

A 2021 study by Ma, Graham and Stettler found relatively small short-term changes after the original ULEZ was introduced in 2019. Average NO2 reductions across monitoring sites were below 3%, and there was no significant overall effect on PM2.5 during that period.

A later five-year prospective cohort study compared children in London with children in Luton. It found faster decreases in NO2 exposure and greater lung-function growth among children in London. The authors interpret this as an association with ULEZ implementation, rather than evidence from a randomised experiment. [10, 11]

The two studies look at different outcomes and time periods, so it's worth keeping them separate rather than saying that ULEZ immediately reduced both nitrogen dioxide and particulate pollution significantly.

Other research cards mention urban heat, crowded housing, mental health, respiratory and coronary disease, and differences in life expectancy across London neighbourhoods. I haven't matched every claim to its original source yet, so these remain research leads.

## Further questions

A few directions I'd like to explore further:

- **Urban green space:** How can cities incorporate more nature into residential areas and public infrastructure?
    
- **Environmental inequality:** How do income, ethnicity and location influence access to nature?
    
- **Accessibility:** What actually makes an outdoor space usable and welcoming for different people?
    
- **Health inequalities:** How does unequal access to nature connect to wider differences in health and wellbeing?
    

For now, I'm keeping these questions together because they overlap quite a bit. They could become separate research notes as I find more evidence.
