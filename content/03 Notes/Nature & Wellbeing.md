# Nature & Wellbeing

I'm interested in what spending time in nature can mean for mental wellbeing, and whether people experience those benefits differently.

A recurring theme in the research is that simply being outdoors might not explain the whole picture. How often we visit green spaces, how connected we feel to nature and how we experience our surroundings could all play a part.

## Greenspace visits & mental health

### Does visiting nature make a difference?

Chang et al. (2024) explored the relationship between public greenspace visits, connection to nature and mental health.

The study found that people who visited public green spaces more frequently generally reported lower levels of stress, anxiety and depression. However, this relationship varied depending on how connected people felt to nature. [1]

People with a stronger connection to nature appeared to experience greater mental-health benefits from frequent visits.

For participants in the lowest quartile of nature connectedness, frequent visits showed little reduction in stress and anxiety. Depression followed a similar pattern, although the interaction wasn't statistically significant. [1]

Interestingly, the amount of time people spent in public green spaces during the previous week wasn't consistently associated with mental-health outcomes.

This makes me wonder whether frequency of visits tells us more than duration, or whether the way someone experiences a place matters just as much as how long they spend there.

### About the study

Chang et al. surveyed 2,084 adults in Brisbane and Sydney, Australia, between April and May 2021.

The researchers measured three main things:

- **Nature connectedness:** Using the experience subscale of the Nature Relatedness scale.
    
- **Mental health:** Using the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21).
    
- **Nature contact:** Frequency of public greenspace visits, time spent in public greenspaces, and visits to private gardens or yards.
    

Participants completed an online survey once, making this a cross-sectional study.

The same study also looked at why people visited green spaces. Its findings about noticing natural features are explored in [[Nature Connection]]. [1]

There are some important limitations to keep in mind. The study identifies associations rather than showing that visiting green spaces directly improves mental health. It also relies on self-reported information from two Australian cities, collected during the pandemic period.

## Nature & psychological wellbeing

### What happens when we experience nature?

Bratman et al. (2019) reviewed research exploring the relationship between nature experience and mental health.

Rather than focusing on one particular environment or activity, the paper brings together findings from psychology, public health, ecology and environmental research. [2]

The authors identify several areas where nature experiences have been associated with positive outcomes:

- **Emotional wellbeing:** Positive emotions, happiness and subjective wellbeing.
    
- **Stress:** Reduced psychological and physiological indicators of stress.
    
- **Cognition:** Improvements in attention, memory and aspects of cognitive functioning.
    
- **Social wellbeing:** Positive social interactions, engagement and a sense of connection.
    
- **Sleep:** Associations between nature experience and improved sleep.
    
- **Mental health:** Research linking nature exposure with lower risks of certain mental-health conditions. [2]
    

The review also discusses experimental studies where viewing images of nature and listening to natural sounds produced positive psychological and physiological responses.

This suggests that some benefits associated with nature can come from indirect experiences, without necessarily being physically present in a natural environment. [2]

That said, looking at a photograph of a landscape and spending time in a woodland are very different experiences. The effects, intensity and duration may vary, so I wouldn't treat them as interchangeable.

### Nature exposure & attention

The review also explores how nature might support cognitive functioning.

Research into attention restoration suggests that natural environments can provide a different kind of stimulation from the demands of everyday life. This may help restore directed attention and reduce mental fatigue. [2]

I'm interested in how smaller, everyday encounters with nature might fit into this. Noticing a tree, watching birds or looking at flowers might seem insignificant, but could these moments influence how we feel and pay attention to our surroundings?

The research raises the possibility, although it doesn't establish that every small encounter will produce a measurable improvement.

### A pathway from place to wellbeing

Bratman et al. describe a framework that separates four stages:

1. **Environmental features:** The qualities of a place, such as biodiversity, structure, composition, size and connectivity.
    
2. **Exposure:** Whether someone can access and encounter nature, including proximity, time and likelihood of exposure.
    
3. **Experience:** How someone interacts with nature, whether visually, physically, intentionally or passively, including what they hear and see.
    
4. **Mental-health outcomes:** The psychological and physiological effects that are measured.
    

I find this distinction useful because it separates the qualities of a place from whether someone actually reaches it, how they experience it and what outcomes might follow.

Having green space nearby doesn't necessarily mean someone will use it, and visiting somewhere doesn't tell us much about the quality of that experience. The framework helps organise the research, rather than suggesting that any particular environment or encounter will automatically produce a specific benefit. [2]

This also connects with Chang et al.'s findings. Their study suggests that frequency of visits and individual nature connectedness may matter together, while time spent in green spaces didn't show a consistent relationship with mental-health outcomes.

The authors discuss encouraging meaningful engagement with nature as a possible planning implication, but their survey didn't test whether a particular design intervention would achieve this. [1]

## A distinction worth keeping

The research here suggests that nature contact isn't just about the amount of time someone spends outdoors.

Frequency, the quality of an experience and how connected someone feels to nature may all be relevant. A visit and an attentive encounter aren't necessarily the same thing, although the available findings don't yet establish exactly how attention influences mental-health outcomes.

I'm interested in this distinction because it shifts the question from simply getting people outside to thinking about what might make an encounter with nature meaningful.

This connects to the questions I'm exploring in [[HELIO®]], particularly the idea of encouraging people to notice their surroundings. However, these studies don't test a particular prompt, product or outdoor target, so they shouldn't be treated as evidence for a specific intervention.

## Further questions

- Does the frequency of nature visits matter more than the amount of time spent outside?
    
- How does nature connectedness influence the benefits people experience?
    
- Can small, everyday encounters with nature support attention and emotional wellbeing?
    
- Do visual representations of nature produce similar effects to direct outdoor experiences?
    
- How do the quality and type of natural environment influence wellbeing?
    
- What makes an encounter with nature feel meaningful rather than passive?
    
- Could encouraging people to notice their surroundings change how they experience time outdoors?
