# Nature & Language

I've been exploring how language shapes the way we understand the world, particularly how we describe and relate to nature.

The words we use can influence what we notice, how we interpret things and where we see ourselves in relation to the natural world. I'm interested in what happens when nature becomes less present in everyday language, and whether the words, metaphors and stories we use can shape our sense of connection to it.

## Linguistic relativity

Linguistic relativity is the idea that the language we speak can influence how we think and perceive the world. It's often associated with the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, which has been debated extensively in linguistics and psychology.

Language doesn't necessarily determine how we think, but it can act as a kind of lens, influencing how we organise and interpret our experiences. [1, 2]

Some of the examples explored in this research include:

- **Colour:** Different languages divide and categorise colours in different ways, which can influence how speakers distinguish and remember them.
    
- **Grammatical gender:** Assigning masculine or feminine grammatical genders to objects can influence the adjectives people associate with them.
    
- **Time and space:** The structure of a language can influence how people imagine time, direction and spatial relationships. [1, 3, 4]
    

### Language, framing & perception

It's not just the words themselves that matter, but how we put them together.

The way an event is described can influence how people interpret it, including who they believe is responsible and what kind of response they think is appropriate.

Alpuim and Ehrenberg (2023) explore how language influences thought, emotion and behaviour. For example, describing something as a natural disaster, an invasion or a crisis can create very different associations.

Language doesn't dictate how someone responds, but it can shape the framework through which they understand a situation. [2]

## Nature vocabulary

I've been interested in the idea that our everyday language is becoming less connected to the natural world.

### The disappearance of nature words

Richardson (2025) explores changes in human-nature relationships between 1800 and 2020, using historical trends in nature-related language as part of a wider model of nature connectedness.

The model estimates a 61.5% decline in nature connectedness over this period. It also uses nature-related word frequency as a proxy for historical changes. That word-frequency measure reached a maximum decline of 60.58% around 1990, before recovering slightly to 52.39% below its earlier level by 2020.

These are two different measurements: the modelled estimate of nature connectedness and the frequency of nature-related words. The word-frequency data isn't a direct record of how connected people felt. [5, 11]

The graph I saved from Grist, titled _Nature is disappearing from our language and our lives_, compares nature-related word frequency in Google Books with a simulation of human-nature interactions.

Both lines decline through much of the twentieth century, although the word-frequency line begins to rise again towards the end. The simulation is a model, rather than another set of historical observations, so the visual relationship doesn't prove that changes in language caused people to become disconnected from nature. [5, 6]

I'm curious whether the language we use to describe nature influences how much attention we give it in everyday life.

Some of the reporting around Richardson's research suggests that declining nature vocabulary and reduced contact with the natural world may reinforce one another. It's an interesting possibility, although the model doesn't establish a direct cause-and-effect relationship. [5, 6, 7]

### The Oxford Junior Dictionary

Another example of this discussion is the Oxford Junior Dictionary.

In a 2007 revision, a number of nature-related words were removed. An Oxford Mail article from 2015 reported that around 50 words had disappeared, including acorn, bluebell, buttercup, conker, minnow and otter.

Some technology-related words appeared instead, including broadband, chatroom, cut and paste, and MP3 player.

The changes prompted criticism from writers including Michael Morpurgo, Margaret Atwood and Andrew Motion. An open letter signed by 28 writers raised concerns about the place of nature in children's language and imagination.

OUP's response, as reported at the time, was that dictionaries should reflect the language children actually use and that the Junior Dictionary was intended as an introductory resource. [8, 9]

I find this particularly interesting because it raises a question about how children learn to recognise and describe the world around them. If certain words become less familiar, does that affect how much attention we pay to the things they describe?

The controversy doesn't prove that removing words from a dictionary causes children to become less connected to nature. It does, however, open up a wider discussion about language, childhood and cultural memory.

Some of the nature words highlighted in the reporting included:

- Acorn
    
- Bluebell
    
- Conker
    
- Dandelion
    
- Fern
    
- Heron
    
- Ivy
    
- Kingfisher
    
- Newt
    
- Willow
    

The list is interesting in itself, but it doesn't tell us whether children are actually less familiar with these species. That's something I'd like to explore further.

### Nature disappearing from culture

Yoder (2025), writing for Grist, brings together Richardson's research with other studies exploring how nature is represented in language and popular culture.

One of the interesting points is that nature may be disappearing from more than just everyday vocabulary. Research by Kesebir and Kesebir (2017) found declining references to nature in English-language books, song lyrics and film storylines since the 1950s.

Yoder also discusses research into how the way we describe nature has changed. Descriptions that once used words such as _lofty_, _stately_ and _noble_ appear to have become less common, alongside a shift towards more scientific and economic ways of talking about the natural world.

This raises an interesting distinction: are we simply talking about nature less, or are we also changing the way we value it?

The article also explores how industrialisation, technology and changing leisure habits might contribute to this growing distance from nature. These ideas connect to the wider concept of intergenerational transmission, where experiences and attitudes towards nature are passed from parents to children.

If one generation has fewer opportunities to experience nature, the next may grow up with fewer opportunities to develop that connection themselves. This could create a cycle where nature becomes less familiar over time. [6, 12]

### Reconnecting through words and storytelling

Yoder also discusses _The Lost Words_ (2017), a book by Robert Macfarlane and Jackie Morris created in response to the Oxford Junior Dictionary controversy.

Through poetry and illustration, the book brings attention back to nature words that had disappeared from the dictionary. It offers an interesting example of how creative work can help reconnect language, imagination and the natural world.

Rather than simply documenting the disappearance of nature vocabulary, it explores how words and storytelling might help bring that familiarity back.

I'm interested in this idea of using creativity to make nature more present in everyday culture, particularly for children. Could storytelling, illustration and design help people notice things they might otherwise overlook? [6]

## Metaphors & cultural relationships with nature

The way we describe nature can also influence how we understand our relationship with it.

Some descriptions frame nature as something to control, extract from or manage. Others place more emphasis on relationships, care, reciprocity and responsibility.

### Nature as a resource

Describing nature as a machine, resource or frontier can position the natural world as something separate from human life, available for use or management.

I'm interested in how this language appears in discussions around ownership, conservation and environmental exploitation. The metaphors we use can influence whether we see nature as something we're part of, or something that exists outside of us.

### Relational perspectives

Bach and Larson (2017) explored how Indigenous Elders and land managers in the Kimberley region of Western Australia describe invasive plants.

The study found that the Aboriginal groups involved often preferred language centred on health, care and creation, rather than the aggressive language of invasion and warfare commonly used in environmental management.

It offers an interesting example of how changing the language around an environmental issue can also change the way people approach it. [10]

It's important to note that this was a specific study involving five Aboriginal groups, not a representation of all Indigenous cultures.

My original Miro research also included references to APIRS (2017), Web of Proceedings (2020), Frickova (2015) and Atman (2024), although I haven't been able to recover their full publication details. I'm keeping these as leads rather than using them to support a wider cultural comparison.

### Mother Nature

Another idea I want to explore is the personification of nature, particularly through the expression 'Mother Nature'.

Giving nature a human identity can create associations with care, nurturing and life. It also raises questions about the role of gender in how we describe and understand the natural world.

Why do we so often associate nature with femininity? And how might these associations influence the way we think about the environment?

I'd like to look further into the relationship between gendered language, cultural symbolism and environmental thinking.

These questions also connect to [[HELIO®]], although the research here doesn't establish whether particular language or prompts can change people's behaviour.

## Further questions

- How does language influence the way we perceive and interact with nature?
    
- What is the original method behind the reported decline in nature-related vocabulary?
    
- Could reduced familiarity with nature words affect children's ability to recognise and identify wildlife?
    
- How do metaphors influence environmental attitudes and behaviour?
    
- How do different cultures describe their relationship with the natural world?
    
- What role does gender play in the way we personify nature?
    
- Could language and storytelling help people develop a stronger connection with their surroundings?
    
- How do historical word-frequency datasets account for changes in language, publishing and the meaning of words?
    
- Can creative work help restore nature's place in everyday language and culture?
    
- How do parents, schools and communities pass nature knowledge between generations?
