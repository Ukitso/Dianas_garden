# Wellbeing as a Cultural Trend

The Miro board gathered several commercial and industry reports about how wellbeing is discussed across generations and in everyday life.

I'm interested in how ideas about health and wellbeing are changing, and how those ideas show up in the things we buy, the spaces we visit and the way we organise our lives.

I'm keeping these examples as cultural signals and reported survey findings, separate from clinical evidence about health.

## Different ideas of health

A Future Laboratory screenshot of an Euromonitor 2020 Health and Nutrition Survey report states that mental wellbeing was a leading meaning of health for:

- 66% of Baby Boomers and Generation X.
    
- 64% of Millennials.
    
- 61% of Generation Z. [1]
    

The screenshot doesn't provide the sample, question wording or full survey citation, so these percentages still need checking against the original report.

The same research material collects examples of wellbeing becoming part of retail, hospitality, fitness and community spaces. These include:

- **Selfridges' Superself:** An initiative featuring sensory mental-health pods by SENSIKS.
    
- **Coa:** Group classes and therapy.
    
- **Bristol Beer Factory's Clear Head:** A low-alcohol drink developed in partnership with Talk Club.
    
- **The Anxiety Shop:** A wellbeing-focused retail concept in Melbourne.
    

These examples show how different brands and services are framing wellbeing as part of everyday life. They illustrate a cultural and commercial shift, but don't establish whether the individual offerings are effective.

The Future Laboratory article also reports a 7% annual growth rate for the global market in memory- and cognitive-health supplements between 2015 and 2020, with the US driving growth.

The screenshot doesn't provide the underlying market dataset, so I'm treating this as a commercial market estimate rather than a measure of clinical need or product effectiveness. [1]

## Work, connection and everyday pressures

The Future Laboratory's _Beauty, Health & Wellness Futures 2023_ material and newer LS:N Global reports point towards a broader set of concerns around wellbeing.

These include burnout, workload, digital fatigue, financial pressure, preventative health, workplace mental-health support, belonging and social connection. The visible cover identifies the 2023 report, while the Euromonitor survey figures above are explicitly from 2020. [2]

LS:N Global's _Generations: Now and Next 2024–2025_ explores themes including:

- Connection and loneliness.
    
- Fluid identities.
    
- Sustainability.
    
- Health and money.
    
- Technology.
    
- Life stages and purpose.
    
- Future aspirations. [3]
    

I found its challenge to simple generational stereotypes particularly useful. People within the same age group aren't interchangeable, and broad generational labels can sometimes hide more than they explain.

These are editorial trend-report themes, rather than representative estimates unless a specific survey is cited. [3]

### Wellbeing as a lifestyle

The LS:N Global article _Connected Healing_ looks at longevity as an everyday lifestyle, social wellness as a way to build connection and long-term practices as an alternative to quick fixes. It features Dr Jonathan Leary of Remedy Place. [4]

I'm interested in how these ideas overlap with nature, community and the pressure to optimise wellbeing.

There's a tension here between making wellbeing more accessible and turning it into another thing to buy, track or improve. I don't think every encounter with nature or every healthy habit needs to become a product or a measure of personal progress.

## A distinction worth keeping

These reports offer a glimpse into how wellbeing is being positioned culturally and commercially. They show the language brands and trend researchers use to talk about health, connection and everyday life.

But there's a difference between what people say they value, what companies sell as wellbeing and what actually supports someone's health.

I'd like to keep that distinction in mind when exploring how design might contribute to wellbeing without simply adding another product, obligation or quick fix.

## Questions I'm following

- How do people of different ages define health and wellbeing for themselves?
    
- When does a wellbeing service offer meaningful support, and when is it mainly a brand promise?
    
- How do financial stress, work, digital life and loneliness shape people's capacity to care for themselves and others?
    
- Why is wellbeing increasingly presented as a lifestyle or consumer choice?
    
- How can design support wellbeing without creating another metric, obligation or quick fix?
    
- Can nature and community offer forms of wellbeing that aren't centred on consumption?
