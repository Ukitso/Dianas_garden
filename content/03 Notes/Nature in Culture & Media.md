# Nature in Culture & Media

I’m interested in how nature appears in the stories, images and everyday media around us—and whether those representations shape what people notice, value or want to experience.

## Nature in cultural products

Kesebir and Kesebir analysed English-language fiction, song lyrics and film storylines across the twentieth century and later. They found that references to nature had declined from the 1950s onward, while references to human-made environments did not show the same pattern. They suggest cultural products may help transmit values and awareness, but the analysis does not show that fewer references directly cause weaker nature connection or environmental concern. [1]

Richardson's later agent-based model used nature-word frequency in cultural products as a historical proxy for nature connectedness. This links the two lines of research, but the proxy and the model are not direct historical surveys of people's feelings. The model's simulated pathways and future scenarios should remain distinct from measured trends. [2]

## Media as both distance and invitation

The material I collected points in two directions. More screen-based leisure may displace some everyday nature experiences; at the same time, nature in films, television, music and social media can inspire people to seek it out. A reported UK survey commissioned by Super, Natural British Columbia found that 70% of respondents said seeing nature on screen inspired them to go outdoors, while a third of Gen Z respondents said they did not see enough nature in media. The survey methodology and representativeness are not available in the package, so these remain reported survey results rather than general facts. [3]

An LS:N Global article also frames nature as aspirational and culturally relevant to younger audiences through outdoor clothing, group activities, birdwatching and visual storytelling. It mentions Flock Together as an example of a birdwatching collective for people of colour. The article attributes to Emily Rhodes (2024) a survey figure that 55% of Gen Z respondents were “very” or “extremely” worried about climate change; the sample and survey method are not shown in the package. Other sticky-note observations included gorp-core and outdoor apparel, fast-fashion fatigue, soft activism and eco-positivity. These are cultural and industry observations, not evaluations of the impact of those initiatives. [4]

One synthesis map frames a possible tension: Gen Z may be worried about climate change and attracted to outdoor culture while still encountering nature mainly through images or lifestyle aesthetics. I’m keeping this as a question raised by the material, not a proven description of a whole generation.

The Miro notes also propose that photographs or other indirect encounters with nature might support attention or wellbeing. Research discussed in the nature-and-mental-health literature includes visual nature exposure, but a mediated image is not the same experience as time in a living environment. I’m keeping this as a question about how digital media may act as a bridge as well as a distraction. [5]

## Language, memory and cultural change

The discussion of nature words and metaphors is developed in [[Nature & Language]]. The Oxford Junior Dictionary controversy, the Richardson word-frequency model and the questions about whose language and ecological knowledge are preserved all connect to this cultural strand. A source attributed on the board to “Yoder (2025)” makes additional claims about vocabulary, cultural feedback loops and *The Lost Words*, but its full reference is missing. I have kept those claims as an unverified lead rather than merging them with Richardson's study. [6]

## Questions I’m following

- Do cultural products reflect changing relationships with nature, help create them, or both?
- Can media inspire outdoor engagement without turning nature into an aesthetic or a lifestyle product?
- How do different communities see themselves represented in nature media and outdoor culture?
- What kinds of nature imagery invite curiosity, care or participation?
- How do digital experiences compare with direct sensory encounters?
