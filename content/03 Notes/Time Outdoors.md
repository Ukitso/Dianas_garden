# Time Outdoors

I've been collecting research about how often people spend time outdoors, how frequently they visit green spaces and how much contact they have with nature.

I'm interested in the difference between simply being outdoors and actively spending time in natural environments. How often we go outside, how long we stay and what we actually do while we're there are all slightly different things.

## How often do people go outside?

### Visits to green and natural spaces

Natural England's Monitor of Engagement with the Natural Environment (MENE) survey provides a useful picture of outdoor activity among adults in England.

The 2018/19 survey found that:

- 65% of adults spent time in the natural environment at least once a week.
    
- 16% went outside at least daily.
    
- 26% went outside several times a week.
    
- 23% went outside once a week.
    
- 16% visited less than once a month or never. [1]
    

The survey also recorded around 90 visits per person each year, equivalent to approximately 1.7 visits per week. [1]

These figures refer to leisure time outdoors, away from home. The survey included places such as parks, canals, nature areas, countryside, woodland, hills, rivers and beaches. Routine shopping trips and time spent in private gardens weren't included in its definition of a visit. [1]

### Changes over time

MENE tracked outdoor visits between 2009 and 2019. The proportion of adults spending leisure time outdoors at least once a week increased from 54% in 2009/10 to 65% in 2018/19. [1]

So, within this particular measure, reported outdoor visits actually increased over the decade rather than declined.

It's worth keeping in mind that these figures measure how frequently people visit natural environments, not the total amount of time they spend outdoors. They also don't tell us how people experience those visits.

## How much time do people spend outdoors?

A separate figure appears in research communication from Loughborough University.

Yvanna Todorova (2025) reported that the average UK person spends around 7% of their time, or approximately 86 minutes, outdoors during the working week. [2]

I haven't yet established the original dataset or calculation behind this figure, so I'm keeping it separate from the MENE statistics.

The two measures aren't directly comparable anyway. MENE looks at the frequency of leisure visits to natural environments, while the 86-minute figure appears to describe time spent outdoors more generally.

I'd like to trace the original research before drawing any conclusions from it.

## Is there a recommended amount of time in nature?

### The two-hour finding

White et al. (2019) investigated the relationship between weekly nature contact, health and psychological wellbeing.

Using data from almost 20,000 people in England, the researchers found that people who spent at least 120 minutes in nature per week were more likely to report good health and higher psychological wellbeing than those who reported no nature contact. [3]

The time could be accumulated across several shorter visits or one longer visit. The association was observed across different demographic groups.

Natural England discussed these findings in a 2019 article titled _Two Hours a Week Outside Is Important for Health and Wellbeing_. The original URL contains the wording 'two-hours-a-day', but the article itself consistently refers to two hours per week. [3, 4]

It's an interesting finding, but it doesn't establish that 120 minutes is a universal requirement or a guaranteed threshold for better health. The study identifies an association, rather than proving that reaching this amount of time causes better outcomes.

## Frequency, duration & nature connectedness

Chang et al. (2024) found that visit frequency and an individual's connection to nature were associated with mental-health outcomes, while visit duration wasn't consistently related to those outcomes. [5]

The study is explored in more detail in [[Nature & Wellbeing]].

This adds another layer to the question of how much time we spend outside. It suggests that frequency and individual relationships with nature may be worth considering alongside duration.

It doesn't establish a universal minimum amount of outdoor time. Any proposed target for [[HELIO®]] remains a project decision, rather than a scientific threshold.

## COVID-19 and everyday nature

Natural England's People and Nature Survey offers a snapshot of how people's outdoor habits and relationship with nature changed during the pandemic.

In March 2022, 68% of adults in England said they were taking more time to notice and engage with everyday nature since the pandemic began. In the same survey release:

- 26% said they had spent no time in green or natural spaces in the previous 14 days.
    
- This rose to 37% among respondents in households with an income below £15,000.
    
- 45% of adults said they were spending more time outdoors than before the pandemic. [6]
    

These figures describe what people reported at that point in time, rather than a permanent change in behaviour for everyone.

The Miro research also records estimates that 36% reported spending more time outside during the first lockdown, increasing to 46% by mid-2020, alongside an increase in park use during summer 2020.

I haven't matched those earlier percentages to their exact survey waves or question wording, so I'm keeping them as provisional rather than combining them with the March 2022 figures. [6]

## Gen Z, screens and outdoor time

An LS:N Global article reports on a survey of 2,000 British adults commissioned by Super, Natural British Columbia.

It suggests that:

- 67% of Gen Z respondents and 57% of Millennials often remained indoors for days.
    
- Around a quarter of respondents across generations made a conscious effort to go outside daily.
    
- 43% of parents thought their children spent less time outdoors than they had at the same age.
    
- Screen time was the most-cited reason, followed by safety, weather and busy schedules.
    
- A third of Gen Z respondents wanted to see more nature in media.
    
- 70% said seeing nature on screen inspired them to go outside. [7]
    

I find the last two figures particularly interesting. Screens are often discussed as something that keeps people indoors, but nature in media might also encourage people to seek out those experiences for themselves.

It's a tension worth exploring, especially when thinking about how culture and digital media influence our relationship with the natural world. This also connects to [[Nature in Culture & Media]].

The figures come from a commissioned survey reported in a trend publication. The full methodology and representativeness weren't available in the material I received, so they should be treated as indicative rather than definitive.

## A distinction worth keeping

One thing that keeps coming up in this research is that outdoor time isn't a single, straightforward measure.

A person might spend a lot of time outside without visiting a natural environment. Someone else might make shorter but more frequent visits to a park or woodland. And even when two people visit the same place, their experiences of it might be quite different.

Frequency, duration, access and the quality of an experience all tell us something different. I'd like to understand how these measures fit together, particularly when thinking about whether small, everyday encounters with nature can make a difference.

## Further questions

- What is the original dataset behind the 7% or 86-minute outdoor-time statistic?
    
- How do researchers distinguish time outdoors from time spent in nature?
    
- Does frequency of visits matter more than duration?
    
- What makes an outdoor experience meaningful rather than simply another visit?
    
- Is there evidence for a minimum amount of nature exposure?
    
- How do people's reasons for going outside influence their experience?
    
- Can everyday encounters with nature have an impact even when they don't involve a dedicated visit to a green space?
