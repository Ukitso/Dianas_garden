# Outdoor Play

I've been interested in how children experience outdoor play, and how their opportunities to explore, move and spend time outside have changed over time.

Play is an important part of childhood. It gives children room to experiment, develop physical skills, interact with others and learn about the world around them. I'm particularly interested in how changes to public spaces, school environments and attitudes towards childhood independence might be affecting these experiences.

## Changes in outdoor play

### Are children playing outside less?

Research collected by the Raising the Nation Play Commission suggests that children now spend around 50% less time playing outdoors than a generation ago. This estimate is attributed to the Real Play Coalition. [1, 2]

The Commission also found that fewer than three in ten children say they play on the street. Its report points to a wider decline in opportunities for unstructured outdoor play. [1]

### Playground closures

A Freedom of Information exercise sent to local authorities identified 429 playground closures across England between 2012 and 2022. The actual number may be higher, as not every authority provided a response. [2]

The Commission also reported that more than two million children in England aged up to nine do not live within a ten-minute walk of a playground. [1]

These figures make me wonder how changes to public spaces are affecting children's ability to play independently, explore their neighbourhoods and spend time outside close to home.

## Barriers to play

The Raising the Nation Play Commission identifies several factors that can limit opportunities for outdoor play:

- **Public spaces:** Playground closures, poorly maintained facilities and a lack of suitable places to play.
    
- **Traffic and safety:** Busier roads and concerns about children playing independently.
    
- **Attitudes towards play:** Restrictions on ball games, signs discouraging play and the perception of children playing outdoors as a nuisance.
    
- **School environments:** Reduced breaktimes and fewer opportunities for unstructured play during the school day.
    
- **Technology:** More time spent indoors using phones, games and other digital media. [1, 3]
    

The Commission describes this wider attitude as an 'anti-play culture'. It argues that children have fewer opportunities to play freely in public spaces, with play increasingly restricted in schools and residential areas. [1, 3]

### School playtime

The Commission also looked at changes in school breaktimes using four national surveys from 1995, 2006, 2017 and 2021.

Between 1995 and 2021, average daily breaktime fell by:

- 23 minutes for the youngest primary school children.
    
- 18 minutes for older primary school children.
    
- 17 minutes for Key Stage 3 pupils. [1]
    

The decline was particularly pronounced in more deprived primary schools. Schools with higher proportions of pupils receiving Free School Meals also tended to have shorter breaktimes. [1]

This raises questions about whether children have equal opportunities to play, even within the school environment.

## Why outdoor play matters

Play gives children opportunities to develop physically, socially and emotionally.

The Raising the Nation Play Commission highlights several developmental benefits:

- **Physical development:** Movement, coordination, strength and physical confidence.
    
- **Social skills:** Cooperation, negotiation and learning to interact with others.
    
- **Emotional development:** Self-regulation, confidence and resilience.
    
- **Cognitive development:** Creativity, memory and problem-solving.
    
- **Independence:** Making decisions, exploring surroundings and learning to assess risk. [1]
    

The British Children's Play Survey (2020) offers another perspective. It collected responses from 1,919 parents and caregivers of children aged 5 to 11 across Britain.

The study found that around half of children's play took place outdoors. Playgrounds and green spaces were among the most common places children played outside the home. It also found that children were generally allowed to play outside independently at an older age than their parents had been. [4]

### What children say they want

A National Trust and First News survey collected responses from 1,000 children aged 7–14 and 1,000 parents in the UK.

It reported that:

- 76% of children wanted more time in nature.
    
- 56% wanted better access to nature and green spaces.
    
- 63% of parents took their children outdoors once a week or less.
    
- Among lower-income parents, 31% cited cost as a barrier. [5]
    

These are survey responses as reported by the organisations. They offer a useful snapshot of this sample, but shouldn't be treated as universal preferences.

The Miro board's 24% figure is now matched to WWF's _Schools for Nature_ report. I've kept the sample details, differences between school years and inequality findings in [[Access to Nature]] under School opportunities rather than repeating them here. [6]

One 2015 article about the Oxford Junior Dictionary controversy also repeated figures suggesting that regular outdoor play in nature had fallen from 40% a generation earlier to 10%, and that 40% of children never played outdoors.

However, the original survey or study behind these figures wasn't identified in the screenshot. I'm keeping them as an unverified source lead rather than treating them as established trend estimates. The dictionary debate itself is explored in [[Nature & Language]]. [7]

## Childhood & nature connection

Outdoor play is one of the ways children become familiar with the natural world.

Climbing trees, collecting objects, exploring woodland and playing in parks all create opportunities to interact with natural environments. These experiences can involve curiosity, movement, imagination and direct contact with nature.

I'm interested in what happens when children have fewer opportunities for these experiences. Could this affect their familiarity with the natural world, or the relationship they develop with it as they grow older?

This connects to wider questions around [[Nature Connectedness]], access to green spaces and the role of outdoor education. It also makes me curious about how our relationship with nature develops beyond childhood.

This is relevant to the ideas I'm exploring in [[HELIO®]], although the research here doesn't tell us whether childhood outdoor play directly influences adult nature connectedness.

## Further questions

- What is the original research behind the estimated 50% decline in outdoor play?
    
- How have children's opportunities for independent play changed across generations?
    
- How do traffic, parental concerns and public attitudes influence outdoor play?
    
- What impact do shorter school breaktimes have on children's development?
    
- Does regular outdoor play influence children's long-term relationship with nature?
    
- How does access to green spaces affect opportunities for unstructured play?
    
- What role do schools and local communities have in creating opportunities for outdoor play?
