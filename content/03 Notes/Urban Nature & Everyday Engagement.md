# Urban Nature & Everyday Engagement

I'm interested in the ordinary, local ways people encounter nature, especially in cities where access, time, safety and space can shape what's possible.

Nature engagement doesn't have to mean a long trip or a remote landscape. It can happen in parks, on everyday routes, in shared gardens or simply by noticing the wildlife and plants around us.

## Everyday urban activities

There are lots of different ways to notice, care for or spend time with nature in an urban environment. Some examples from my research include:

- **Noticing wildlife:** Birdwatching, wildlife photography, plant and fungi identification, and citizen-science recording.
    
- **Growing things:** Herbs, vegetables and flowers in windows, balconies, rooftops or shared gardens, including planting for pollinators and wildlife.
    
- **Exploring local spaces:** Visiting city farms, canals, parks and community gardens, or simply noticing birds, insects, trees, fungi and resilient plants in built-up areas.
    
- **Urban growing and food:** Foraging, urban beekeeping, mushroom cultivation, aquaponics and hydroponics, where safe and appropriate.
    
- **Water-based activities:** Canoeing, swimming, lidos, pedal boats and other ways of encountering urban waterways.
    
- **Slower encounters:** Mindful walks, cloud watching and creative ways of documenting overlooked nature.
    

These are examples and prompts rather than a ranked list of activities or evidence that any one of them improves wellbeing.

Some of the original material came from lifestyle websites, forums and commercial guides, so claims about popularity, growth or ecological benefits need stronger sources before I treat them as findings. In particular, claims that city bees face fewer pesticides or produce "higher quality" honey weren't substantiated in the material I collected.

## Community, culture and access

I'm also interested in how people find ways to engage with nature together, and whether outdoor activities feel welcoming to people who might not traditionally see themselves represented in outdoor culture.

The Miro research points towards group birdwatching, community stewardship and more culturally inclusive activities. Flock Together is one example mentioned in an LS:N Global article. The National Trust and First News report also suggested practical ways to widen participation, including subsidised activities and events in local parks or urban farms. These are potential approaches, rather than evidence that particular interventions have been tested and shown to work. [1, 2]

Access depends on more than motivation. Time, transport, cost, distance, maintenance, safety, accessible paths and facilities, pollution and whether people feel welcome can all shape the choices available to someone.

I've explored some of these wider barriers in [[Access to Nature]].

Richardson's 2025 model also proposes ways to strengthen nature connectedness across generations, including family support, school curricula, community-led stewardship and substantial urban greening. These are policy ideas and proposed future scenarios, not evaluations demonstrating that each intervention works. [3]

## Questions I'm following

- Which local activities feel welcoming to people who haven't traditionally seen themselves represented in outdoor culture?
    
- How can community programmes reduce practical barriers such as cost, transport and safety?
    
- What makes an urban encounter feel meaningful: attention, familiarity, social connection or something else?
    
- How can cities make ordinary routes and public spaces more biodiverse and accessible?
    
- How can digital prompts help someone notice nearby nature without turning it into a task or a metric?
    
- What role can local communities play in caring for urban nature?
