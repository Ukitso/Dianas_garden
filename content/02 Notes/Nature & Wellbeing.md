# Nature & Wellbeing

I'm interested in what nature contact can mean for mental wellbeing, and whether the experience differs between people.

## Greenspace visits & mental health

### Does visiting nature make a difference?

Chang et al. (2024) explored the relationship between public greenspace visits, connection to nature and mental health. The study found that people who visited public green spaces more frequently generally reported lower levels of stress, anxiety and depression. However, the relationship varied depending on how connected people felt to nature. [1]

People with a stronger connection to nature appeared to experience greater mental-health benefits from frequent visits.

For those in the lowest quartile of nature connectedness, frequent visits showed little reduction in stress and anxiety. Depression followed a similar pattern, although the interaction was not statistically significant. [1]

Interestingly, the amount of time spent in public green spaces during the previous week was not consistently associated with mental-health outcomes.

This suggests that frequency of visits and duration of exposure may not tell the whole story. How people experience and engage with nature could also matter.

### About the study

Chang et al. surveyed 2,084 adults in Brisbane and Sydney, Australia, between April and May 2021.

The researchers measured:

- **Nature connectedness:** Using the Nature Relatedness scale, specifically its experience subscale.
    
- **Mental health:** Using the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21).
    
- **Nature contact:** Frequency of public greenspace visits, time spent in public greenspaces, and visits to private gardens or yards.
    

Participants completed an online survey once, making this a cross-sectional study.

The same study examined why people visited green spaces. Its findings about noticing natural features are in [[Nature Connection]]. [1]

There are some important limitations. The study identifies associations rather than proving that visiting green spaces causes improvements in mental health. It also relies on self-reported information from two Australian cities, collected during the pandemic period.

## Nature & psychological wellbeing

### What happens when we experience nature?

Bratman et al. (2019) reviewed research exploring the relationship between nature experience and mental health. Rather than focusing on one particular environment or activity, the paper brings together findings from psychology, public health, ecology and environmental research. [2]

The review identifies several areas where nature experience has been associated with positive outcomes:

- **Emotional wellbeing:** Positive emotions, happiness and subjective wellbeing.
    
- **Stress:** Reduced psychological and physiological indicators of stress.
    
- **Cognition:** Improvements in attention, memory and aspects of cognitive functioning.
    
- **Social wellbeing:** Positive social interactions, engagement and a sense of connection.
    
- **Sleep:** Associations between nature experience and improved sleep.
    
- **Mental health:** Research linking nature exposure with lower risks of certain mental-health conditions.
    

The authors also discuss experimental studies in which viewing nature images and listening to natural sounds produced positive psychological and physiological responses. This suggests that nature experiences do not always require direct physical contact with a natural environment. [2]

However, looking at a photograph of a landscape and spending time in a woodland are different types of experiences. The effects, intensity and duration may vary.

### Nature exposure & attention

The review also explores how nature experience might support cognitive functioning.

Research into attention restoration suggests that natural environments can provide a different kind of stimulation from the demands of everyday life. This may help restore directed attention and reduce mental fatigue. [2]

I'm interested in how relatively small encounters with nature, such as noticing a tree, watching birds or looking at flowers, might influence how we feel and pay attention to our surroundings.

## A distinction worth keeping

The research here doesn't reduce nature contact to time spent outdoors. Frequency, quality of experience and individual connection may also matter. A visit and an attentive encounter are not necessarily the same thing, though the available findings don't yet establish how attention changes mental-health outcomes.

That distinction is useful to the questions I'm exploring in [[HELIO®]], but these studies don't test a particular prompt, product or outdoor target.

## Further questions

- Does frequency of nature visits matter more than the amount of time spent outside?
    
- How does nature connectedness influence the benefits people experience?
    
- Can small, everyday encounters with nature improve attention and emotional wellbeing?
    
- Do visual representations of nature produce similar effects to direct outdoor experiences?
    
- How do the quality and type of natural environment influence wellbeing?
