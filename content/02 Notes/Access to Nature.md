# Access to Nature

Access to nature is about more than having a green space nearby. The quality of that space, how safe it feels, whether it is physically accessible and whether people feel welcome all influence how it is used.

I've been interested in how access to green space differs between communities, and how factors such as income, location, disability and cultural background can shape people's experiences of the natural world.

## Barriers to access

### Distance, quality & safety

Having a park nearby doesn't necessarily mean people can use or enjoy it.

Some of the research I've collected explores:

- **Location:** Distance from green spaces and the availability of nature within residential areas.
    
- **Quality:** Maintenance, biodiversity, facilities and the condition of public spaces.
    
- **Safety:** Fear of crime, antisocial behaviour, poor lighting and feeling unsafe.
    
- **Physical accessibility:** Step-free routes, accessible paths, seating and public facilities.
    
- **Belonging:** Whether different communities feel represented, comfortable and welcome in outdoor spaces.
    

These barriers can affect people differently, particularly disabled people, older adults, women, families and marginalised communities. [1, 2]

### Social & economic barriers

Access is also shaped by everyday circumstances.

Work, commuting, caring responsibilities and limited free time can make it difficult to spend time outdoors. Transport costs, lack of nearby facilities and cultural familiarity with particular spaces can create further barriers. I haven't matched a source to each of these points yet.

I'm particularly interested in the difference between having access to green space in theory and being able to use it in practice.

## Environmental inequality

Access to green space is not distributed equally. Income, ethnicity and where people live can all influence the amount and quality of nature available to them. [3, 4]

Some of the reports I've collected explore the relationship between deprivation and access to green space, including the idea of urban areas with very little accessible nature, sometimes described as 'grey deserts'. [3, 4]

This raises questions about who benefits from urban green spaces, whose neighbourhoods receive investment and whether access to nature is treated as an essential part of public infrastructure.

## Nature, health & urban environments

I've also collected research leads connecting access to green space with wider health inequalities.

The topics include:

- Mental health and psychological wellbeing.
    
- Air pollution and environmental exposure.
    
- Physical health and opportunities for activity.
    
- Differences in life expectancy between neighbourhoods.
    
- Health inequalities in deprived urban communities.
    

The Mental Health Foundation has a related overview of nature and mental health. [5] Organisations and research groups mentioned alongside other claims include The King's Fund, the Institute of Health Equity, British Heart Foundation, Friends of the Earth, Parks for London and Imperial College London's Environment Research Group. ## Further directions

- **Urban green space:** How cities incorporate nature into residential areas and public infrastructure.
    
- **Environmental inequality:** How income, ethnicity and location influence access to nature.
    
- **Green-space accessibility:** What makes an outdoor environment genuinely usable and welcoming?
    
- **Health inequalities:** How unequal access to nature relates to wider differences in health and wellbeing.
    

These could become individual research notes as the material develops. For now, I'm keeping them together because the themes overlap.
