# Nature & Language

I've been exploring how language shapes the way we understand the world, particularly how we describe and relate to nature.

The words we use can influence how we perceive things, what we pay attention to and how we understand our place within the natural world. I'm interested in what happens when nature becomes less present in everyday language, and how different words, metaphors and cultural perspectives might shape our relationship with it.

## Linguistic relativity

Linguistic relativity is the idea that the language we speak can influence how we think and perceive the world. Sometimes associated with the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, it has been debated extensively within linguistics and psychology.

Language isn't necessarily a fixed framework that determines how we think. It may instead act as a lens, influencing the way we organise and interpret experiences. [1, 2]

Research has explored several examples:

- **Colour:** Different languages divide and categorise colour in different ways, which can influence how speakers perceive and remember colour distinctions.
    
- **Grammatical gender:** Assigning masculine or feminine grammatical genders to objects can influence the adjectives people associate with them.
    
- **Time and space:** Languages can influence how people imagine time, direction and spatial relationships. [1, 3, 4]
    

### Language, framing & perception

The words used to describe an event can also influence how people interpret it.

Alpuim and Ehrenberg (2023) explore how language influences thought, emotion and behaviour, including the way grammatical structures assign responsibility and how particular words frame situations.

For example, describing something as a natural disaster, an invasion or a crisis can create different associations and suggest different responses. Language doesn't determine how someone thinks, but it can influence the framework through which they interpret something. [2]

## Nature vocabulary

I've been interested in the possibility that our everyday language is becoming less connected to the natural world.

### The disappearance of nature words

A study by Miles Richardson (2025) explores changes in human-nature relationships between 1800 and 2020. It uses historical trends in nature-related word use within cultural products to model changes in nature connectedness.

The research suggests a substantial decline in nature-related language over time. One figure associated with this research is a decline of over 60%, although I still need to establish the exact calculation and which word categories it represents. [5]

I'm curious whether the language we use to describe nature influences how much attention we give it.

Some of the reporting around this research suggests a relationship between reduced nature vocabulary and declining contact with the natural world. A possible feedback loop is worth exploring, but the model does not establish that one directly causes the other. [5, 6, 7]

### The Oxford Junior Dictionary

In 2007, a revised edition of the Oxford Junior Dictionary removed a number of nature-related words, including:

- Acorn
    
- Bluebell
    
- Conker
    
- Dandelion
    
- Fern
    
- Heron
    
- Ivy
    
- Kingfisher
    
- Newt
    
- Willow
    

Some were replaced by words associated with technology and modern childhood, such as blog, broadband and voicemail. The changes attracted criticism from writers and naturalists, including Robert Macfarlane and Margaret Atwood. [8, 9]

The debate raised questions about how children's vocabulary reflects their surroundings and experiences.

It also connects to a wider concern about whether children are becoming less familiar with the plants, animals and landscapes around them. However, dictionary changes alone don't demonstrate a decline in outdoor play or nature connection.

## Metaphors & cultural relationships with nature

The language we use to describe nature can influence how we understand our relationship with it.

Some metaphors position nature as something to control, extract from or manage. Others describe it through relationships of care, reciprocity, kinship and responsibility.

### Nature as a resource

Descriptions of nature as a machine, resource or frontier can frame the natural world as something separate from human life, available for use or management.

This is particularly interesting when looking at environmental language around ownership, conservation and exploitation.

### Relational perspectives

Bach and Larson (2017) examined how Indigenous Elders and land managers in the Kimberley region of Western Australia describe invasive plants.

Their research found that the Aboriginal groups involved often preferred language and metaphors centred on health, care and creation, rather than the aggressive language of invasion and warfare commonly used in environmental management.

The study demonstrates how different metaphors can influence environmental management practices and the way people relate to ecological problems. [10]

This is one specific study involving five Aboriginal groups, rather than a general representation of all Indigenous cultures.

### Mother Nature

Another idea I'm interested in is the personification of nature through language, particularly the expression 'Mother Nature'.

Giving nature a human identity can create associations with care, nurturing and life. It also raises questions about how gender influences the way we describe and understand the natural world.

I'd like to explore this further, particularly the relationship between gendered language, cultural symbolism and environmental thinking.

These questions about wording also connect to [[HELIO®]], though the sources here don't test whether a particular prompt changes behaviour.

## Further questions

- How does language influence the way we perceive and interact with nature?
    
- What is the original method behind the reported 60% decline in nature-related vocabulary?
    
- Could reduced familiarity with nature-related words influence children's ability to recognise and identify wildlife?
    
- How do metaphors influence environmental attitudes and behaviour?
    
- How do different cultures describe their relationship with the natural world?
    
- What role does gender play in the way we personify nature?
    
- Could language and storytelling help people develop a stronger connection with their surroundings?
